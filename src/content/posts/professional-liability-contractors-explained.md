The line between construction work and professional services is blurring. Contractors today routinely provide design-build services, write specifications, manage projects, advise clients on materials and methods, and take on contractual responsibility for outcomes that extend well beyond physical labor.

General liability insurance was designed for the physical work. Professional liability insurance — also called errors and omissions (E&O) — was designed for everything else. The gap between them is where expensive claims happen.

## What General Liability Actually Covers

GL covers third-party bodily injury and property damage arising from your operations. The coverage is triggered by something physical: a worker injures a bystander, you damage a client's property while working, a structural failure injures someone.

GL explicitly excludes **professional services** — decisions, advice, designs, specifications, and professional judgment. The exclusion is broad and intentional. It's not an oversight; it reflects the fact that professional liability risk is underwritten differently and priced separately.

When a client sues you because your design was wrong, your specification was defective, your project management created a cost overrun, or your professional advice led to a loss — that's a professional liability claim. Your GL policy won't respond.

## When Contractors Need Professional Liability

The trigger is simple: if any part of your work involves making professional decisions beyond physical labor, you carry professional liability exposure.

**Design-build contractors** are the most obvious case. When you're responsible for both the design and the construction, you carry the full professional and physical risk. A design error that leads to a structural failure, a water intrusion problem, or a code violation is a professional liability claim against the design component.

**Contractors who write specifications.** Even if you don't do the design, writing specifications for materials, methods, or systems is a professional act. If the specification is wrong and the project fails, the claim is against your professional judgment.

**Construction managers and project managers.** If you're coordinating subcontractors, scheduling, managing a project budget, and making decisions about the project's execution without physically performing the work yourself, you're providing professional services. A scheduling failure that causes a cost overrun, a coordination failure that creates a defect — these are professional liability claims.

**Contractors who consult.** Some contractors advise clients on what to build, how to build it, or what vendors to hire before any physical work begins. That advisory role is entirely professional in nature.

**Contractors with technology systems.** Contractors who install integrated systems — building automation, smart home technology, energy management — often carry professional exposure for the commissioning and configuration of those systems.

## The Risk Is Growing

The trend in construction contracts is toward contractor accountability for outcomes, not just inputs. Performance specifications (build it so it achieves X result) put professional responsibility on contractors in ways that prescriptive specifications (install product Y per method Z) do not.

Design-build is growing as a delivery method. More project owners want single-point responsibility — they want one contractor accountable for both design and construction. That's efficient for the owner and lucrative for the contractor, but it means carrying the full professional exposure.

GC subcontracts increasingly include professional liability insurance requirements for subcontractors who provide any design or consulting component. If you're winning work with design-build or consulting elements, your GC is probably requiring E&O coverage in the subcontract.

## What Professional Liability Covers

E&O insurance covers claims arising from:

- **Errors in professional work** — mistakes in designs, specifications, calculations, or professional advice that result in a financial loss to the client
- **Omissions** — failures to do something you were professionally obligated to do
- **Negligent acts** — professional work performed below the standard of care for your profession
- **Defense costs** — legal fees and litigation costs for defending a professional negligence claim, even when you're not found liable

The coverage pays:
- Your defense costs (which can run $50,000–$300,000+ for a complex construction dispute)
- Judgments and settlements up to the policy limit
- In some forms, rectification costs — the cost of fixing the professional error

## Claims-Made Structure

Most professional liability policies are **claims-made**, not occurrence. This is an important distinction from GL.

Under a claims-made policy, the claim must be filed and reported while the policy is active for coverage to apply. If your policy lapses or you switch carriers, claims arising from past professional work may not be covered unless you purchase a tail extension (also called an extended reporting period).

This creates a planning requirement: if you discontinue professional liability coverage — because you've retired, sold the business, or changed focus — you need a tail policy to maintain coverage for prior work. Tail policies are available but add cost.

## How Much Coverage Do You Need?

Professional liability limits are often set independently of GL limits. Common contractor E&O limits are $1M per claim / $1M aggregate for smaller firms, scaling to $2M–$5M for larger operations or firms with significant design-build exposure.

The right limit depends on:
- The dollar value of your projects
- The potential cost of a serious design error on your largest project
- What your GC subcontracts or client contracts require
- Whether umbrella can sit above the E&O limit (most umbrellas are GL-only; professional liability excess is separate)

## Coordinating GL and Professional Liability

The practical goal is a program where GL and E&O together cover the full range of your operations with no gaps between them.

The challenge is where the line sits between physical work and professional services. When a design flaw leads to a physical failure that injures someone, is it a GL claim (physical injury) or a professional liability claim (design error)? The answer often depends on how the claim is pleaded — and a sophisticated plaintiffs' attorney will plead it both ways, naming both policies and hoping at least one responds.

We structure GL and E&O programs to coordinate well at that boundary. Coordinated coverage means the coverage territory is defined, the limits are appropriate on both sides, and there's no ambiguity about which policy responds to which type of claim.

## What to Ask Before Binding Professional Liability

**What professional services does your scope include?** The policy should match what you actually do — design-build, specification writing, consulting, project management.

**Is the policy claims-made? What's the retroactive date?** The retroactive date determines how far back prior work is covered. A policy with a retroactive date of "today" only covers future work; you want the retroactive date to go back to when your professional exposure began.

**What are the deductibles?** E&O deductibles are typically higher than GL deductibles — $5,000–$25,000 per claim is common. Factor that into your decision.

**Does the policy include defense costs inside or outside limits?** Some E&O forms erode your coverage limit with defense costs. Others pay defense outside the limit, preserving the full limit for judgments and settlements.

## The Bottom Line

GL is not enough for contractors who do more than physical work. Professional liability isn't optional for design-build firms, project managers, specification writers, or consultants — it's the coverage that protects the professional judgment component of what you do.
