Most contractors carry a $1 million per-occurrence / $2 million aggregate general liability policy. That's the standard starting point — a baseline that satisfies most certificate requirements for routine work.

But a standard $1M GL isn't enough for catastrophic claims, high-value commercial projects, or GC subcontracts that increasingly require $5M in total liability limits. That's where a commercial umbrella comes in.

## What a Commercial Umbrella Policy Does

A commercial umbrella sits above your primary insurance policies and responds when those underlying limits are exhausted. The most common underlying policies in a contractor umbrella program are:

- General liability (GL)
- Commercial auto liability
- Employers liability (Part Two of workers' compensation)

When a claim exhausts your primary GL limit — say, a $2M construction defect judgment on a $1M GL policy — the umbrella pays the next $1M, $2M, $5M, or more, depending on your umbrella limit.

The umbrella is designed for catastrophic events: a serious injury on a major commercial project, a large construction defect claim with multiple plaintiffs, a catastrophic vehicle accident involving a work truck. These events can generate judgments and settlements that far exceed primary policy limits. Without an umbrella, the business is exposed above those limits.

## How Much Does a Contractor Umbrella Cost?

Commercial umbrella coverage is among the most cost-effective insurance available. A $1M umbrella above a standard contractor GL and auto program typically costs $1,500–$3,500 annually for most contractors. Each additional million in umbrella limit usually costs less than the first million — because the excess layer is less likely to be reached.

A $5M umbrella above primary GL of $1M/$2M is achievable for $5,000–$15,000 annually for many contractor profiles. Compare that to the alternative: increasing the primary GL limit to $5M would cost far more and provide less (umbrellas cover multiple underlying policies simultaneously; increasing just GL doesn't extend coverage to auto or employers liability).

## GC Subcontract Requirements Are Increasing

The single biggest driver of contractor umbrella purchases isn't a claim — it's a subcontract requirement.

General contractors on commercial, institutional, and public projects increasingly require subcontractors to carry total liability limits of $2M, $3M, or $5M. With a standard $1M GL policy, the only way to meet those requirements is with an umbrella.

A contractor who can't meet a GC's minimum insurance requirements can't bid that project. As projects get larger and GCs manage risk more aggressively, umbrella requirements are migrating down the value scale — requirements that used to apply only to large commercial projects are now appearing on mid-size residential projects, school district work, and municipal contracts.

Review your current subcontract insurance requirements before bidding. If you're already required to carry $3M or $5M in total limits, you need an umbrella to meet those requirements.

## Follow-Form Coverage: What the Umbrella Actually Covers

Most commercial umbrellas are **follow-form** policies, which means they follow the terms, conditions, and exclusions of the underlying GL policy. If something is covered by your GL, it's covered by the umbrella above it. If something is excluded from your GL, it's typically excluded from the umbrella as well.

This has important implications:

**Pollution:** If your GL contains a pollution exclusion (most do), the umbrella typically won't cover pollution claims either. Contractors with pollution exposure need separate environmental liability.

**Professional liability:** Umbrellas are not professional liability policies. E&O coverage doesn't extend through a standard umbrella.

**Employment practices:** EPLI (employment practices liability) is not extended by a standard commercial umbrella.

For these specialty lines, separate coverage is required regardless of umbrella limits.

## Additional Insured Status Through the Umbrella

Many GC subcontracts require that the GC be named as an additional insured on both the primary GL and the commercial umbrella. This is standard language — and it's important to verify that your umbrella policy allows additional-insured endorsements.

Some umbrella policies limit or exclude additional-insured status. Others allow it but require the primary policy to include an additional-insured endorsement first (which is standard). Make sure your umbrella is structured to allow AI endorsements at the umbrella layer when your contracts require it.

## Umbrella vs. Excess Liability: What's the Difference?

These terms are often used interchangeably but they're technically different:

**Umbrella:** Typically follows form (matches primary policy terms) AND can sometimes fill gaps by dropping down to cover claims not covered by primary (subject to a self-insured retention). More flexible.

**Excess liability:** Simply adds limits above the primary policy with the same terms. No drop-down. Less flexible.

For contractors, a true follow-form umbrella is generally preferable. But in practice, what carriers offer and what the market calls an "umbrella" varies. Review the actual policy form with your agent.

## How the Umbrella Interacts With Completed Operations

The umbrella extends above your GL's completed-operations coverage as well. A large construction defect claim that runs past your primary GL completed-operations aggregate has the umbrella available to respond.

This is particularly important for contractors with significant project values. A single large commercial project where a construction defect claim involves multiple property owners, extensive investigation, and complex litigation can easily exhaust a $1M or $2M primary GL limit. The umbrella is what protects the business above that.

## Maintenance Requirements: Keeping the Underlying Limits Active

Umbrella policies have underlying limit requirements — minimum primary limits that must be maintained to trigger the umbrella. If your GL lapses, your auto lapses, or your limits drop below the umbrella's requirements, the umbrella may not respond — or may respond only after you satisfy the gap with your own funds.

The most common maintenance requirement is:
- GL: $1M/$2M minimum (per occurrence / aggregate)
- Commercial auto: $1M combined single limit minimum
- Employers liability: $500K/$500K/$500K minimum

Keep your primary policies in force with at least these limits to ensure the umbrella responds properly.

## When to Review Your Umbrella Limits

Your umbrella limit should be reviewed when:
- You take on a new contract that requires higher total liability limits
- Your project values have grown significantly
- You've added trucks or fleet vehicles
- You've expanded to commercial projects from residential
- You've added employees and your employers liability exposure has grown

Most contractors who first purchase a $1M umbrella find themselves needing $2M or $3M within a few years of growing their operations.

## The Practical Case for a Contractor Umbrella

An umbrella is not a luxury purchase for large contractors. It's a practical risk management tool that:

1. **Satisfies GC subcontract requirements** that standard primary limits can't meet
2. **Protects the business** from catastrophic claims that exceed primary limits
3. **Costs very little relative to the coverage provided** — often less than 5% of total program premium for 300–500% more liability capacity

If you've never carried an umbrella, or if your current umbrella limit was set years ago when your projects were smaller, it's worth a review.
