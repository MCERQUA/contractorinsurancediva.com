Workers' compensation premium is calculated on payroll — specifically, on payroll by class code. Class codes are the numerical identifiers that define each type of work an employee performs and determine the rate applied to that payroll. Get them right and your premium reflects your actual exposure. Get them wrong and you either overpay for years or face an audit that bills you the difference — plus penalties — after the fact.

Understanding class codes is one of the highest-value things a contractor can do to manage workers' comp costs.

## How Class Codes Work

The National Council on Compensation Insurance (NCCI) maintains the system used in most states. Each class code has a corresponding rate — expressed as a dollar amount per $100 of payroll. A high-hazard trade with significant injury risk carries a higher rate; a low-hazard class like office staff carries a much lower rate.

Your annual premium is: **Payroll ÷ 100 × Rate × Experience Modification Factor**

If your payroll is $1,000,000, your workers' comp rate is $8.00 per $100, and your experience mod is 1.0, your premium is $80,000. If the right code should have been $5.00 per $100, you're overpaying $30,000 per year.

This is why correct classification matters.

## Common Class Codes for Contractor Trades

Here are the NCCI codes most frequently used for contractor operations. State variations exist — California, Texas, New York, and several others operate their own bureau systems with slightly different codes. Consult your agent for your state's specific codes.

**General construction and carpentry:**
- **5645** — Carpentry (residential construction, interior trim, framing)
- **5651** — Carpentry (all other, non-residential)
- **5403** — Carpentry (not otherwise classified, cabinet installation)

**Electrical:**
- **5190** — Electrical wiring, residential
- **5191** — Electrical wiring, commercial
- **5183** — Electrical wiring, industrial (often carries a higher rate)

**Plumbing and HVAC:**
- **5183** — Plumbing (residential)
- **5184** — Plumbing (non-residential)
- **5537** — HVAC and refrigeration (installation, service)

**Masonry and concrete:**
- **5022** — Masonry (brick, stone, concrete block)
- **5213** — Concrete work (pouring, flatwork, foundations)
- **5221** — Concrete construction, not otherwise classified

**Painting and drywall:**
- **5474** — Painting (all operations)
- **5480** — Plastering and drywall (installation, taping, finishing)
- **5482** — Plastering — not otherwise classified

**Roofing:**
- **5551** — Roofing (all types, residential and commercial)
- **5545** — Concrete or cement construction, roofs

**Flooring:**
- **5348** — Hardwood flooring (installation)
- **9521** — Floor covering — tile or carpet (installation)

**Landscaping and site work:**
- **0042** — Landscape gardening
- **6229** — Excavation, grading, land clearing
- **6233** — Pile driving

**Office and sales staff:**
- **8742** — Outside sales (no physical work)
- **8810** — Clerical office employees

## The Most Common Class Code Mistakes

**Using a single code for the whole crew.** A mixed crew with framers, electricians, and office staff has meaningfully different rate exposure across those roles. Running everyone under the highest-rated trade code is easy but expensive. Segregating payroll by code is worth the administrative effort.

**Using the wrong residential vs. commercial code.** Residential and commercial work often carry different codes and different rates. A plumbing crew that does both should have payroll segregated accordingly.

**Missing executive officer classifications.** Most states have a special code for executive officers (owners and principals) with a fixed payroll cap that limits premium. Not using this code — or not excluding owners who are not required to carry coverage — means overpaying.

**Misclassifying subcontractors as employees.** If you hire 1099 subcontractors and they don't have their own workers' comp certificates on file, carriers often add their payroll to your audit at the highest-rated class code on your policy. Require certificates from every sub and keep them on file.

## The Experience Modification Factor

After three years of coverage, your experience mod is calculated by comparing your actual losses to the expected losses for your payroll and codes. If you've had fewer claims than average for your trades, your mod goes below 1.0 (reducing your premium). If you've had more claims, it goes above 1.0 (increasing your premium).

A 0.80 experience mod saves you 20% on base premium. A 1.30 mod costs you 30% more. For a $1M base premium, that's a $300,000 swing depending on claims history.

One serious lost-time claim — a fractured spine, a traumatic brain injury, a severe burn — can move your experience mod for three years. The claim doesn't have to be your fault to count in the mod calculation. This is why safety programs, proper classification, and proactive return-to-work programs are financially significant, not just regulatory requirements.

## At Audit, Wrong Codes Create Problems

Workers' comp policies are audited annually. The carrier compares the payroll you reported to actual payroll records — by class code. If you've been coding everyone under a lower-rated code, the audit reclassifies and bills you the difference.

Audit surprises from misclassification are one of the most common and avoidable problems in contractor workers' comp. They can result in:

- Significant additional premium bills due immediately
- Policy cancellation if the additional premium isn't paid
- Non-renewal if the exposure profile doesn't match what was submitted

The best protection is accurate classification up front, verified by an agent who knows contractor class codes across trades.
